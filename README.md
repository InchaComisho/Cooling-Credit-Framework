## Overview

The **Cooling Credit Framework** is a proposed environmental credit system that assigns measurable value not only to carbon reduction, but also to actions that **directly reduce heat loads** in cities, soils, forests, agricultural lands, oceans, buildings, and regional water systems.

Current climate policy is largely centered on carbon accounting: how much CO₂ is emitted, reduced, captured, or offset.
This approach remains important. However, climate damage ultimately appears as **heat**: extreme temperatures, heat islands, droughts, wildfires, ocean warming, heat stress, crop failure, increased cooling demand, and ecological disruption.

Therefore, climate action must evolve from a carbon-only framework into a broader heat-response framework.

This framework proposes the following principle:

> Climate action should evaluate not only how much CO₂ has been reduced,
> but also how much heat has actually been reduced,
> how much water circulation has been restored,
> and how much natural cooling capacity has been regenerated.

The Cooling Credit Framework does not reject carbon credits.
Instead, it expands climate evaluation into areas that carbon credits alone cannot fully capture: thermal inertia, accumulated ocean heat, urban waste heat, degraded soil moisture, reduced vegetation transpiration, and broken water cycles.

In short:

> **Carbon credits evaluate carbon accounting.
> Cooling credits evaluate real-world thermal reduction and cooling contribution.**

---

## Background: Why Carbon Credits Alone Are Not Enough

Carbon credits are designed to assign value to activities that reduce, remove, or offset greenhouse gas emissions.
They are mainly based on CO₂-equivalent accounting.

This is useful, but insufficient.

Global warming is not experienced by society as a spreadsheet of carbon values.
It is experienced as accumulated heat.

The Earth system has thermal inertia.
The ocean, in particular, has an enormous heat capacity. Once excess heat enters the ocean, land, atmosphere, and built environments, its effects persist with delay and are not immediately reversed even if emissions are reduced.

Cities also accumulate heat through:

* air conditioner exhaust
* asphalt and concrete surfaces
* building walls and rooftops
* industrial heat discharge
* data centers
* transportation infrastructure
* reduced green space
* limited water retention

Agricultural and forest systems also lose cooling capacity when soil organic matter declines, forests are removed, vegetation becomes simplified, and water retention decreases.

Therefore, climate action requires two complementary approaches:

1. **Input-side measures**
   Measures that reduce additional greenhouse gas emissions and limit future heat trapping.

2. **Output-side measures**
   Measures that directly reduce accumulated or locally retained heat and restore natural cooling pathways.

Carbon credits primarily evaluate the first category.
Cooling credits are designed to evaluate the second.

---

## Definition: What Is a Cooling Credit?

A **Cooling Credit** is a measurable credit assigned to an activity that reduces heat load, restores water-cycle-based cooling, or improves local and regional thermal conditions.

In simple terms:

> **A Cooling Credit gives value to actions that actually cool the environment.**

Cooling credits may apply to:

* cities
* buildings
* agricultural land
* forests
* soils
* wetlands
* oceans
* water infrastructure
* cooling devices
* heat-reduction technologies
* direct planetary cooling systems

The goal is not to create a new form of permission to emit CO₂.
The goal is to create a system that rewards actual cooling contribution.

## Core Principle: Water Is the Cooling Medium of the Earth

The Cooling Credit Framework is centered on one fundamental insight:

> **Water is not merely a resource.
> Water is the primary cooling medium of the Earth system.**

Most natural cooling processes depend on water.

Rain cools the ground.
Water absorbs heat as it evaporates.
Plants draw water from the soil and release it through transpiration.
Moist soil prevents extreme surface heating.
Forests create shade and cool the air through evapotranspiration.
Clouds reduce incoming solar radiation.
Rain returns water to the surface and sustains the cycle.

This sequence forms one of the Earth’s natural cooling systems.

When water circulation collapses, cooling capacity also collapses.

Dry soil heats rapidly.
Bare ground stores heat.
Cities without vegetation become heat islands.
Deforested landscapes lose transpiration.
Degraded agricultural land loses moisture.
Stagnant oceans lose oxygen and biological productivity.
Urban systems expel heat without restoring local cooling cycles.

Therefore, climate policy must ask not only:

* How much CO₂ was reduced?

but also:

* How much water was retained?
* How much evapotranspiration was restored?
* How much soil moisture was recovered?
* How much urban heat was reduced?
* How much surface temperature was lowered?
* How much cooling demand was avoided?
* How much natural cooling function was regenerated?

Cooling credits are designed to answer these questions.

## Technical Evaluation Model

To implement cooling credits, cooling effects must be measured, estimated, and verified.

The following simplified models provide an initial technical structure.

---

## 1. Evaporative Cooling

When water evaporates, it absorbs heat from the surrounding environment.
The cooling effect can be estimated from the amount of evaporated water and the latent heat of vaporization.

```text
Q_evap = m_evap × L_v
```

Where:

* `Q_evap` = evaporative cooling energy
* `m_evap` = mass of evaporated water
* `L_v` = latent heat of vaporization of water

This model applies to:

* mist cooling
* soil evaporation
* plant transpiration
* water-retentive pavement
* urban waterways
* wetland restoration

---

## 2. Sensible Heat Reduction

A reduction in air or exhaust temperature can be estimated using air density, specific heat, airflow rate, temperature difference, and operating time.

```text
Q_sensible = ρ × c_p × V_dot × ΔT × t
```

Where:

* `Q_sensible` = sensible heat reduction
* `ρ` = air density
* `c_p` = specific heat capacity of air
* `V_dot` = airflow rate
* `ΔT` = temperature reduction
* `t` = operating time

This model applies to:

* air-conditioner outdoor-unit exhaust
* factory exhaust
* data-center heat discharge
* urban ventilation
* mechanical cooling systems

---

## 3. Surface Temperature Reduction

Surface temperature can be measured using satellite data, infrared cameras, or ground-based sensors.

```text
ΔT_surface = T_baseline - T_project
```

Where:

* `T_baseline` = surface temperature before intervention or in a control area
* `T_project` = surface temperature after intervention or in the treated area

This model applies to:

* water-retentive pavement
* urban greening
* agricultural soil restoration
* rooftop greening
* forest restoration
* cool roofs

---

## 4. WBGT Reduction

Human heat risk cannot be evaluated by air temperature alone.
Humidity, solar radiation, and wind also matter.

```text
ΔWBGT = WBGT_baseline - WBGT_project
```

Where:

* `WBGT_baseline` = WBGT before intervention or in a control area
* `WBGT_project` = WBGT after intervention or in the treated area

Because evaporative cooling may increase humidity, WBGT must be evaluated together with temperature.

---

## 5. Cooling Demand Reduction

If a building, district, or city becomes cooler, electricity demand for cooling can decrease.

```text
E_saved = E_baseline - E_project
```

Where:

* `E_saved` = electricity saved
* `E_baseline` = electricity demand before intervention or under baseline conditions
* `E_project` = electricity demand after intervention

This model applies to:

* buildings
* urban greening
* outdoor-unit cooling
* reflective roofing
* district cooling strategies
* water-cycle-based urban cooling

## Basic Credit Calculation Structure

A Cooling Credit Score may be designed as a composite index.

```text
Cooling Credit Score =
  Thermal Reduction
+ Water Cycle Recovery
+ Evapotranspiration Recovery
+ Heat Risk Reduction
+ Energy Demand Reduction
+ Ecological Cooling Recovery
- Water Stress Penalty
- Humidity Risk Penalty
- Ecological Risk Penalty
```

This structure is important because cooling must not be evaluated in isolation.

For example:

* Mist cooling can be effective in dry regions, but may worsen perceived heat in humid conditions.
* Afforestation can restore cooling, but poorly selected species may increase water stress.
* Ocean circulation technologies may support cooling and oxygenation, but must be evaluated carefully for ecological effects.
* Urban water use must not compete with drinking water or ecosystem flow requirements.

Cooling credits must therefore be designed as a **regional, conditional, and verifiable framework**, not as a simple “cooling at any cost” mechanism.

## Relationship with Water-Circulation Cities

The core implementation model of the Cooling Credit Framework is the **Water-Circulation City**.

A Water-Circulation City is an urban model that receives, stores, uses, purifies, reuses, and returns water to soil, vegetation, waterways, and the atmosphere.

Conventional cities import water, food, and energy, consume them internally, and export wastewater, waste, and heat.

A Water-Circulation City reverses this structure.

It:

* receives rainwater
* uses reclaimed water
* returns water to soil
* supplies water to vegetation
* cools through evapotranspiration
* circulates water through waterways
* reduces artificial heat discharge
* lowers cooling demand
* reduces heat-stress risk

In this sense, a Water-Circulation City reconnects urban civilization with the Earth’s natural cooling system.

Cooling credits make this value visible, measurable, and economically actionable.

## Implementation Risks and Design Principles

Cooling credits are promising, but they must be designed carefully.

---

## 1. Humidity Risk

Evaporative cooling can reduce air temperature while increasing humidity.
In humid climates, this may worsen perceived heat.

Therefore, WBGT and human heat stress must be evaluated together with air temperature.

---

## 2. Water-Resource Risk

Cooling should not create water scarcity.
Rainwater, reclaimed water, greywater, and treated wastewater should be prioritized.

---

## 3. Ecological Risk

Forest restoration, ocean circulation support, wetland restoration, and large-scale greening can affect ecosystems.
Regional suitability, native species, biodiversity, and long-term monitoring are essential.

---

## 4. Over-Crediting Risk

Short-term or poorly measured cooling effects should not be overvalued.
Reliable baselines, control areas, seasonal correction, and third-party verification are necessary.

---

## 5. Offset Misuse Risk

Cooling credits must not be used to justify continued emissions.

The rule should be clear:

> Cooling Credits are cooling contribution certificates, not emission offset permissions.

## Cooling Credits Are Not Solar-Shielding Credits

Cooling Credits should not be confused with solar-shielding, solar-radiation-blocking, or stratospheric aerosol schemes.

Blocking sunlight may reduce additional incoming heat, but it does not directly reduce the heat already accumulated in oceans, soils, cities, buildings, infrastructure, and ecosystems.

In this framework, Cooling Credits are defined as measurable contributions to actual heat-load reduction and natural cooling recovery.

Cooling Credit targets include:

- urban heat-load reduction
- waste-heat reduction
- water-cycle restoration
- soil moisture recovery
- vegetation transpiration recovery
- evapotranspiration-based cooling
- ecological cooling recovery
- ocean circulation and surface-cooling support

Solar shielding may be classified as heat-input suppression, but it is not the same as direct planetary cooling.

Cooling Credits are intended to evaluate actions that reduce existing heat load, restore natural cooling functions, and lower thermal stress in real environments.

In short:

> Solar shielding reduces part of future heat input.
> Cooling Credits evaluate measurable reduction of existing heat load and restoration of natural cooling capacity.

## Cooling Credits vs. Solar Shielding — Comparison Table

| Category | Description | Cooling Credit Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Solar shielding | Blocks or reflects incoming sunlight | Not classified as direct cooling by itself |
| Heat-input suppression | Reduces future heat entering a system | Secondary or limited relevance |
| Reflective / insulation measures | Reduces heat absorption by roofs, roads, walls, or buildings | May be evaluated as urban heat-load reduction when measurable |
| Waste-heat reduction | Reduces heat discharged from AC systems, buildings, vehicles, data centers, and industry | Core evaluation target |
| Water-cycle cooling | Uses evaporation, transpiration, soil moisture, and water circulation to release heat | Core evaluation target |
| Soil and forest restoration | Restores water retention, transpiration, carbon fixation, and ecological cooling | Core evaluation target |
| Ocean circulation recovery | Supports surface cooling, oxygenation, and vertical circulation | Core evaluation target |
| Carbon reduction | Reduces future warming drivers | Necessary, but not the primary basis of Cooling Credits |
